Mixed-Methods Evaluation of Immersive Systems

Study design, instrumentation and analysis across the caregiver-training studies

Four psychometric instruments, three network-analysis techniques, and two independent rater-reliability checks, reported across five peer-reviewed papers.

  • The instrument matrix
  • All projects
  • Publications

An immersive system is easy to demo and hard to evaluate. This side of the work pairs standard psychometric instruments with network analysis of what people actually said, so a claim about empathy rests on a validated scale and on the transcript.

In plain terms

Anyone can demo a virtual reality system and report that it went well. Proving it is the hard part.

This strand of the work is about evidence. Every claim made about the simulation — that caregivers found it usable, that it made them more empathetic — has to rest on two independent kinds of measurement: questionnaires other researchers have already validated, and analysis of what people actually said out loud during the session.

The quantitative side

Established instruments, used as published, so the numbers mean something outside this lab.

  • Usability

    System Usability Scale — 10 items, 5-point

    Scored 0–100 by the standard re-scaling. A score of 68 is the accepted average, which gives every result a reference point rather than a bare number.

  • Acceptance

    Technology Acceptance Model — 8 items, 4-point

    The perceived ease-of-use dimension, scored 0–32, with negatively worded items reverse-scored.

  • Empathy

    Jefferson Scale of Empathy — 20 items, 7-point

    Administered before the first interaction and after the last, so it brackets the whole session rather than a single condition.

  • Social presence

    Networked Minds — 36 items, 6 dimensions

    Co-presence, attention, perceived message understanding, perceived affective understanding, and emotional and behavioural interdependence, completed after each condition.

Turning transcripts into structure

Quantitative ethnography: keep the detail of qualitative work, then model and test it.

  • Thematic analysis

    Open-ended survey responses read for recurring patterns: what people liked, what they struggled with, what they asked for.

  • Epistemic Network Analysis

    Which concepts co-occur. Codes become nodes, co-occurrences become weighted edges, and two networks can be subtracted and compared statistically.

  • Ordered Network Analysis

    Which concept follows which. Adds direction to the edges, so a shift from trial-and-error to strategic reasoning becomes visible.

  • CORDTRA

    When things happened. Interaction triggers per minute plotted against session length, as a proxy for engagement over time.

Rigour, concretely

The parts that are easy to skip and expensive to skip.

Effect sizes from the network analyses

Cohen’s d for the difference between two conditions or categories, all from two-sample t-tests on the first dimension. By convention 0.8 is already a large effect.

  • Coding reliability

    Two independent raters, every time

    Interview coding reached Cohen’s κ = 0.863 with 93.7% agreement. Where an automated classifier did the bulk coding, it was validated against two human raters at κ = 0.76 and 0.83 before being trusted with the rest.

  • Sample size decided in advance

    Power analysis, not hindsight

    G*Power was used to check the achieved power for the 16-participant study (0.93 at α = 0.05) rather than reporting significance from whoever turned up.

  • Tests chosen for the data

    Non-parametric where normality is unclear

    With n = 16 the Shapiro-Wilk test left normality in doubt, so the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used instead of a t-test; two-sample t-tests assuming unequal variance were used on the network centroids.

  • Interpretation closed back to the data

    Networks re-read against transcripts

    Structural findings were checked against the actual exchanges that produced them, so an edge in a network can be traced to something a participant said.
