Research · Doctoral dissertation · In progress

Aware Intelligent Virtual Humans in Mixed Reality

New Jersey Institute of Technology · advised by Dr. Salam Daher

Building on the published Wizard-of-Oz system and its three first-author evaluations.

Everything that makes the virtual patient seem aware is, today, a person behind a divider pressing buttons. The dissertation asks whether the system can do it itself, and holds the automated version to the operator’s own standard.

What “aware” means here

Three concrete mechanisms, all currently driven by hand.

  • Remembers the person

    Names and clothing colours are entered into the response database before the session and linked to buttons the operator presses at the right moment.

  • Reads the room

    A lamp and a radio go on and off; the operator works a remote control on the virtual human’s behalf.

  • Carries the thread

    Details from an earlier conversation reappear in a later one, because the operator knows they happened.

Why automating it is the research question

The published papers are candid about what the operator costs. Each of these is a measurable target.

  • Latency

    1–3 seconds typically

    The ISMAR study logged a normal response latency of one to three seconds, with early outliers of five to ten seconds attributed to the operator’s limited practice. An automated controller has a number to beat.

  • Turn-taking

    Speech overlap sits at the operator level

    The same paper places the overlap problem “at the operational level” and notes it can be reduced with operator practice, or solved with a stop-talking mechanism in the system itself.

  • Cost and reach

    One trained operator per session

    The IVA paper argues that removing the human operator would improve cost-effectiveness and make the system easier to deploy across settings.

  • Range

    A fixed pool of pre-recorded replies

    Every study lists the limited response set as a limitation; participants asked for more variety, more specific answers and more organic responses.

The published future-work sections point the same way: an automated response controller trained on the interaction data already collected, and large language models in place of the Wizard-of-Oz operator.

How the automated system will be judged

Two stages, in order.

  • Stage 1 — against the operator

    A benchmark, not a user study

    • Non-verbal response accuracy against the human-controlled baseline
    • System latency against the same baseline
    • Run on the behaviours the operator already produces, so the comparison is like for like

  • Stage 2 — against perception

    A user study

    • Perceived accuracy of the generated behaviour
    • Perceived latency — whether the delay is noticed, not just measured
    • Perceived realism of the resulting interaction

The system as it stands

Where it leads

The through-line

The published studies show that a virtual patient who appears aware changes how caregivers behave: more person-centred care, more strategic reasoning, higher engagement. That effect is currently produced by a person, one session at a time.

If the same behaviour can be generated automatically, at operator-level accuracy and latency, the training stops being a research apparatus and becomes something a nursing programme could actually run.
